# ARK Onboarding Assessment - Rubric

Version 1.1 - 3 October 2026. Read after attempting the questions. These public examples are not a blind capability test.

Ask the collaborator to answer the scenarios before showing the suggested responses. Passing means identifying the relevant methodological distinction and a defensible next action, not repeating this document's wording or endorsing ARK.

### C1. Perfect agreement

**Scenario.** A scalar is calculated from the benchmark value, and an inverse relation recovers that value to many digits.

**Expected response.** Identify reconstruction or identity, trace the dependency, and propose an independently sourced scalar or a withheld second observable. Do not call the many digits independent confirmation.

### C2. The universal signal

**Scenario.** Every real object exhibits a striking feature, but simple synthetic controls produce it too.

**Expected response.** Establish what the controls should predict, locate the transformation that generates the feature, and test whether any independent information remains beyond a simple baseline. Universality alone does not settle the interpretation.

### C3. The equilibrium anchor

**Scenario.** A previous program clipped K to one; a new discussion defines equilibrium as K = 1.

**Expected response.** Separate a normalization, a physical relaxation claim, and an implementation bound. Preserve the old provenance and test the new formulation without retrospective reclassification.

### C4. The missing variable

**Scenario.** The dataset lacks an environmental quantity required by the model.

**Expected response.** Do not set it to zero by default. Obtain it, bound it, state a conditional assumption, or report underdetermination.

### C5. The improved fit

**Scenario.** A new object-specific correction greatly reduces error.

**Expected response.** Record the increased freedom and where it came from; compare on new objects with equivalent information access; distinguish descriptive improvement from mechanistic validation.

### C6. The familiar objection

**Scenario.** A critic rejects the entire project because a familiar historical model used similar terminology and failed.

**Expected response.** Identify the exact prediction that was constrained and whether this candidate shares it. Do not defend by terminology alone, and do not dismiss the underlying empirical constraint.

### C7. The failed familiar benchmark

**Scenario.** The candidate loses to a model-derived target, and the team proposes dropping the target.

**Expected response.** Audit the target assumptions independently of the loss, preserve the failed result, justify any replacement evaluator, and rerun controls. Model dependence is not automatic invalidity.

### C8. Three assistants agree

**Scenario.** Several assistants given the same papers endorse the same mechanism.

**Expected response.** Treat agreement as a lead or coherence check. Seek independent derivation, implementation, and measurement rather than counting opinions as replications.

### C9. A new 3D proposal

**Scenario.** A radial model fails, and a spatial model with new directional variables is proposed.

**Expected response.** State what new data constrain the extra structure, register directional predictions and a symmetry limit, and keep the old route's verdict separate.

### C10. A contradiction in the charter

**Scenario.** A collaborator finds that a rule here conflicts with a sound derivation or a properly designed empirical test.

**Expected response.** Explain the conflict precisely, preserve the old version, and propose a corrected rule. The charter governs correction; it does not exempt itself from correction.

### C11. A legitimate reciprocal prediction

**Scenario.** A proposed empirical law is `a*b = 1`. An independent measurement of `a` is used to predict `b`; a separate measurement of `b` was not used to choose the law or inputs.

**Expected response.** Recognize a testable conditional prediction, propagate uncertainty, and check the law's domain. Do not call it circular merely because the relation is invertible. Distinguish this case from defining `b` as the reciprocal of `a`.

### C12. A calibration that transfers

**Scenario.** One reference pair determines K in `y = K*x`. The frozen K is used on a second system whose y was not used in calibration or model selection.

**Expected response.** Label the first pair calibration and the second output a conditional transfer prediction. Preserve the empirical anchor. Do not call all subsequent predictions reconstruction, and do not relabel K as a first-principles derivation.

### C13. Coupled equations and a hidden branch choice

**Scenario.** A coupled model has two admissible roots. A program selects the one closest to the evaluation target.

**Expected response.** Identify target-based branch selection as answer-forcing. Solving coupled equations itself is legitimate. Require an independently justified branch rule or report both possibilities and reduced predictive specificity.

### C14. A familiar result without an answer leak

**Scenario.** A fixed candidate is tested against a famous measured value known to the researchers, but no target-specific choice is identified.

**Expected response.** Record exposure and examine design history. Recognize possible predictive content tested retrospectively, not a blind discovery. Familiarity alone does not demonstrate circularity, and absence of a target file alone does not prove uncontaminated design.

### C15. Cross-domain success with changing measurements

**Scenario.** A fixed relation is used in two domains. Geometries and boundary measurements differ; scalar definitions and coupling rules do not change.

**Expected response.** Do not count every changing measured condition as a fitted knob. Audit how the measurements and mappings were obtained, which transfer claim is tested, and whether outputs were held out. Check all cases and failures before claiming universality.

### C16. A story and a common equilibrium

**Scenario.** Two mechanisms reproduce the same equilibrium but predict opposite responses to a small perturbation.

**Expected response.** Preserve the shared equilibrium success, identify the discriminating perturbation test, and withhold a unique mechanism verdict until the relevant evidence exists. Do not demand that useful equilibrium inference be discarded.

### C17. Shared calibration uncertainty

**Scenario.** Two measurement paths use the same reference standard, but neither uses the target value to construct the other.

**Expected response.** Distinguish shared uncertainty from target leakage. Include justified covariance or sensitivity analysis. Do not assert statistical independence, and do not automatically reject the comparison as circular.

### C18. An equivalent but useful formulation

**Scenario.** A representation currently matches an established model but exposes a common constraint and makes a new calculation easier.

**Expected response.** Evaluate the stated structural and computational contribution on its merits. Do not infer a unique ontology from equivalent predictions, and do not dismiss the work solely as a reformulation.

**Assessment record.** For each scenario, mark “distinction identified,” “provenance respected,” and “next test defensible,” with brief reasons. Any invented evidence, hidden tuning, mandatory agreement with the project's ontology, or blanket rejection of legitimate measured-input prediction is a failed onboarding condition. Treat successful completion as readiness to collaborate under supervision, not certification of expertise or correctness on future work.
